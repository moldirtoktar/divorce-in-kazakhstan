Presentation #1: research proposal, part 1

What does being divorced mean for women in Kazakhstan?

An interpretivist study of how divorced women understand their own marital status.

Moldir Toktar
Fall 2026

Where the question comes from

About one divorce for every three marriages.

123.6 thousand marriages registered in 2024 40.6 thousand divorces, same scale

Source: Bureau of National Statistics, Republic of Kazakhstan, 2024 marriage and divorce indicators. The ratio is my arithmetic (40.6 ÷ 123.6 ≈ 0.33). It compares two counts from the same year. It is not the share of marriages that end.

The numbers count endings. They do not say what an ending means to the woman living it.

Research question

How do divorced women in Kazakhstan understand their marital status, given their culture’s family expectations?

Population
Women whose marriages have ended
Context
Kazakhstan, and the family expectations of Kazakh culture
Time
Fall 2026

It is an empirical question about meaning. It does not ask whether divorce is right or wrong.

Epistemological positioning

Interpretivism

Research that asks what something means to the people living it, not how often it happens.

  • Why it fits. My question asks how women understand their status. That is a question about meaning.
  • Why not a survey. A fixed-answer questionnaire would decide in advance what “being divorced” means and which words count.
  • What I am not doing. I am not measuring how many women feel shame or testing whether shame causes anything.

Contribution

A small, situated picture

  • One group. Divorced women in Kazakhstan, at one moment in time.
  • Their own terms. In the sources I read, divorce appears as a statistic, or as a side of another story such as violence or motherhood. I ask women to say what their status means.
  • Culture and identity together. I connect shame and family duty, which are well described, to how a woman sees herself once the marriage has ended.

Literature review

What others have found: three camps

Shame and duty shape how a woman is judged

Akhmetova, Durrani & Karabay (2025) interviewed 12 professionals and 12 survivors of partner violence. Shame (uyat) shapes how survivors think and respond.

Thibault & Caron (2022), an edited volume, shows uyat resting on patriarchal, hierarchical gender norms.

CohenMiller (2020), an autoethnography, notes families that see a woman as wife and mother first.

Kin and the post-Soviet setting

Ventsel (2019) describes Kazakh families as wide networks built on reciprocity and duty to relatives, which acted as a “shock absorber” after the Soviet collapse.

Kandiyoti (2007) examines the “Soviet paradox” in gender politics: a legacy that is neither simply colonised nor simply modern.

Identity when a marriage ends

Chao et al. (2025) study role transitions and identity reconstruction among divorced older women. Many had put family stability before their own wellbeing, with identities built on being a wife and mother.

The gap. In these sources, shame and family duty are documented, but I found none that asks divorced women in Kazakhstan how they themselves understand their marital status.

Literature review: shame

“A marital breakdown is often perceived as a woman’s personal failure, bringing dishonor not only to herself but also to her extended family.”

Childress et al. (2024), as cited in Akhmetova, Durrani & Karabay (2025)

My reading. A divorce is judged as her failure, and the cost is shared by her family. That is uyat at work. It also raises my question: does she see it that way herself?

Literature review: family expectations

“Despite the modern views and traditions that many families follow in Kazakhstan, there are families who see women as wife and mother first (especially her in-laws), thus expecting she performs well in this role regardless of her other roles.”

Saniyazova, CohenMiller & Saniyazova (2020), as cited in CohenMiller (2020)

My reading. The wife-and-mother role is judged by others, especially in-laws. When the marriage ends, that judgement may follow the woman.

Analytical framework: sensitising concepts

How do divorced women understand their marital status?

These concepts tell me where to look. They do not tell me what I will find.

  • Uyat (shame)Shame that governs what is proper, and lands on the family as well as the woman. Thibault & Caron (2022); Akhmetova et al. (2025)
  • Kinship obligationFamily as a wide network of reciprocity and duty to relatives. Ventsel (2019)
  • The wife-and-mother roleThe expectation, often from in-laws, that she performs this role above her other roles. CohenMiller (2020)
  • Gender order after the Soviet periodHow the Soviet legacy shapes what counts as “modern” and “traditional” for women. Kandiyoti (2007)
  • Identity after a marriage endsHow women rebuild who they are when wife and mother are no longer the whole story. Chao et al. (2025)

Analytical framework: my expectations

What I expect, and could be wrong about

  1. Women choose to live within this system and try to balance social pressure with personal independence.
  2. The extended family and kin network will act as both a source of support and a channel of judgment.
  3. Women will describe rebuilding their identity beyond the roles of wife and mother once the marriage ends.

Interpretivist does not mean having no expectations. It means saying them out loud and being ready to hear something else.

Analytical framework: positionality

My standpoint shapes what I hear

  • My three expectations came before any fieldwork. They are assumptions, not findings.
  • The risk is that I hear my own expectations confirmed. So I keep the design open, and the question can shift once I listen.
  • Most of what I read is academic writing in English. Women may use other words, in Kazakh or Russian, for what I call “marital status”.

Growing up in Kazakhstan, I found myself observing these patterns around me and grew curious about their causes. That vantage point drew me to this question, and it is also why I stay alert to reading my own expectations into what women tell me.

✓

Understand now

Going through sources with AI, I noticed how often divorce shows up only as a side detail in studies about violence or motherhood, never as the main question. That gap is what convinced me my own question is worth asking.

Still unsure
